# Planning and attendance

Start with the smallest useful outcome, not a standing calendar slot. A status
update may need only a readable message; contested tradeoffs, joint discovery,
coordination dependencies, or relationship work may justify live exchange.
For urgent or sensitive matters, compare the risks of delay, async exposure, and
excluding affected people. Do not call every relationship conversation wasteful
because it lacks a decision artifact.

## Build the invite rationale

Use current authorized colleague context if available: source/date, remit,
relevant knowledge, decision rights, implementation responsibility, and impact.
Mark stale or conflicting information and verify only the gaps that change the
plan. Do not scrape private profiles or disclose sensitive context to fill a grid.
Reporting rank can suggest where to ask, but cannot prove decision authority.

| Participation | Rationale | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Essential | Authorized decision owner, required approval, or knowledge/interdependence needed live | Authority, availability, delegation, relevant agenda segment |
| Optional | Useful context or learning, with a meaningful choice to decline | Whether notes or a limited segment suffice |
| Async | Input/review can reach the discussion without attendance | Accessible channel, adequate window, who incorporates it |
| Informed afterward | Affected by outcome but no contribution needed before it | Whether this wrongly excludes a perspective needed before the decision |

Do not classify impacted people as merely informed by default. Obtain their
perspectives before decisions affecting them; a representative is appropriate
only when representation and mandate are credible. Do not equate seniority,
extroversion, or proximity to the organizer with unique knowledge. Avoid token
attendance without influence. Explain omissions as well as invitations when useful.
If context is sparse, propose role-based slots and ask who holds decision rights;
never invent a named owner. Missing authority may require deferring a decision,
getting delegated authority, or reframing the session as discovery.

## Plan access before requests arrive

Include ordinary accessibility options in every brief and invitation, regardless
of whether any disability is known: accessible advance materials, captions where
available, processing pauses, written/asynchronous input, breaks and flexible
ways to contribute. Check which features are available and usable; state gaps
and alternatives rather than promising support the venue/platform cannot deliver.
Do not add a diagnosis survey to intake. Offer a private route for additional
preferences and accommodations, and keep its details out of shared records.
Check authorized preference memory for verified attendees, including source,
date and scope; apply current relevant preferences without exposing attribution.
A shared starting point does not replace individual support. For conflicting
preferences, work with participants on equivalent access and keep choices flexible.

## Fit the form

Use an agenda that connects each topic to a question/outcome, preparation, and
rough time budget. Timeboxes are adjustable aids, not grounds to suppress dissent
or access needs. State time zone and proposed duration, check working hours,
care commitments, availability and transition time, and rotate recurring burdens
where feasible. Do not promise an optimal universal meeting length or frequency.

For memo reviews, link the exact reviewed version; plan advance or in-session
reading to fit participants and local norms. Use `amazon-memo` when the memo
itself needs work. Do not duplicate its narrative templates or claim that a
particular reading allocation is experimental fact.

For hybrid meetings, test whether remote participants can hear, access materials,
and enter the discussion equally. Name a remote participation monitor if useful
and agreed. Confirm captions/interpreting, venue access, equipment, accessible
material formats and alternatives; avoid making everyone troubleshoot at the start.
