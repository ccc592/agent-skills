# Accessible participation and facilitation

## Start with access for everyone

Organizers generally will not know attendees’ diagnoses. Offer accessibility
power-ups to the whole group as ordinary meeting options: advance accessible
materials, usable captions where available, time to process, written/asynchronous
input, breaks, and flexible participation. Do not wait for disclosure, a named
disability, or a private request before including them in planning and invitations.

Universal design and the curb-cut effect support removing barriers proactively:
features essential for some people can also help others, including people with
situational or temporary barriers. This is a design rationale, not proof that
every option improves every meeting for everyone. Read the evidence reference
for source types and limits. Offer options rather than compulsory modes.

Shared defaults are a starting point, not a substitute for individual
accommodations. Give everyone an optional private request channel, explain who
will see requests, and describe what can actually be provided. Ask what supports
participation without asking for a diagnosis or justification. Reconcile conflicting
preferences with usable alternatives; do not decide whose needs matter by rank,
majority vote, or presumed diagnosis. Do not put private requests or health
information in attendee lists or public minutes.

## Prepare access

- Send a clear purpose, agenda, questions, relevant definitions, and accessible
  pre-reads with enough lead time for the actual group. Offer text rather than
  image-only slides; use headings, descriptive links, readable spacing, and
  alternatives for diagrams. Provide key information in more than one form.
- Offer written input before and after the session with a defined review window
  when appropriate. Extra processing time should not quietly remove someone's
  ability to affect a decision already made. Identify when input is needed and
  how it will be considered.
- Ask about captioning, interpretation, screen-reader access, room access,
  sensory conditions, breaks and participation modes without asking for medical
  proof. For venues, check step-free routes, toilets, travel and hearing/sightline
  access; for platforms, test keyboard access and assistive technology. Offer
  captions to everyone where available and check language, accuracy and platform
  compatibility; automated captions and transcripts can miss names, jargon, accents and speaker changes.
- Consider quiet rooms, predictable structure, controllable lighting/noise,
  flexible seating or movement, a dial-in option, and camera choice. Plan breaks
  and transitions for the duration and group. Do not mandate eye contact,
  immobility, camera use, or visible enthusiasm as evidence of attention. Reconcile
  camera preferences with access to faces, lip-reading or interpreters; an
  interpreter/speaker view need not require everyone to turn cameras on. Camera
  choice does not guarantee less fatigue.

Offer structure, clear priorities, movement, predictable transitions, explicit
language, sensory choices, readable layouts, text-to-speech-compatible materials,
and freedom from surprise reading aloud to anyone who finds them useful. ADHD,
autism and dyslexia workplace evidence helps identify barriers worth designing
for; it is not a reason to branch by diagnosis or presume a personal profile.
Needs vary and overlap. Ask what works and avoid claims of treatment.

Live captions can support access without recording or retaining the meeting.
A caption request or joining the call is not consent to recording, retained
transcription or an AI bot. Explain purpose, audience, retention and alternatives
before authorized capture; if permission is unclear, leave capture off. In shared
invitation access wording, explicitly distinguish available captions from any
recording or retained transcription, which require separate authorization/consent.

## Facilitate live participation

Make agenda changes explicit. Use plain, concrete questions, one thread at a
time, and a visible place for parked questions. Explain jargon. Say who is
speaking, verbalize relevant visual information, and avoid overlapping speech.
Allow a pause before responses; a fast answer is not necessarily a better answer.

For idea generation, offer quiet individual thinking/writing before group
comparison where useful, with a non-writing equivalent if needed. For discussion,
use a clear queue and invite overlooked evidence. Let people pass, contribute in
chat/document/voice, or return later within an agreed window. Avoid mandatory
round-robins, surprise cold-calling, or forcing everyone to tell personal stories.
Do not create simultaneous chat, slide and verbal tasks that overload attention;
make important chat contributions part of the shared discussion.

Leaders should ask questions and demonstrate listening: paraphrase, check
interpretation, explore disconfirming information, and explain how input affected
the outcome. Avoid rigid speaking quotas and do not infer agreement from quietness.
Make disagreement safe to record without requiring public disclosure of why a
participation method is difficult. Humor and informal conversation are optional;
never require a social performance as the price of contributing.

For live interventions, start with a brief actionable sentence and pause; put
process explanation in subsequent turns or the written record. A long corrective
speech can itself impede access. If the decision process permits a unilateral
call, distinguish that call from consensus and preserve any access failure.

Close with explicit decision status, accepted commitments and unknowns. Offer a
correction channel, particularly when captions or transcription were unreliable.
Ask whether the next iteration needs an adjustment without requiring disclosure.

## Remember practical preferences, not medical profiles

When someone expresses a meeting preference, use an available authorized memory
facility to retain and later apply it. This is a workflow requirement, not a
research finding or permission to expand data collection. Verify the person's
identity against authorized context (for example, a stable colleague ID); a first
name alone may be ambiguous. Do not attach a preference to a guessed identity or
another person with the same name. Clarify the identity before persisting or
applying person-specific memory; current-session support can continue meanwhile.

Store only the practical preference, verified identity, source/date, and relevant
scope (for example, remote workshops rather than every interaction). Preserve
whether it is ongoing or event-specific; do not turn a one-off request into a
permanent preference. Exclude diagnoses, medical explanations and unnecessary
quotes, even if volunteered. Keep private visibility and any limits on reuse.
Confirm the facility's authorization and audience before writing; do not use an
arbitrary shared CRM, public file, repository or agenda as substitute memory.

When planning a later meeting, consult authorized preference memory for verified
attendees and apply relevant preferences alongside broad accessibility defaults.
Do not require the person to disclose again merely to use a current preference.
For stale, conflicting or context-sensitive entries, check privately rather than
assuming they override the person's latest statement. Honor later corrections,
scope changes and requests to forget using the facility's supported controls;
record a correction as the new source, with its actual date or date unknown,
without silently losing provenance or inventing a timestamp;
verify the operation before claiming an update or deletion. Do not keep a shadow
copy after forgetting or silently recreate a forgotten preference from old notes.
If a control or memory facility is unavailable, explain that limitation precisely
and do not claim persistence, deletion or future recall. Use current context only
within its permitted scope, and do not treat a request to forget as permission to
keep applying that stored preference.

Shared invitations can describe available options without saying who requested
them or why. Person-linked private preferences belong neither in public skill
examples nor shared agendas/notes. Public examples must be fictional. If support
requires disclosure to a provider or organizer, establish the permitted minimum
and audience before sharing rather than exposing the entire memory entry.
